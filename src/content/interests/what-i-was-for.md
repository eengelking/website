I love [The Egg](http://galactanet.com/oneoff/theegg_mod.html) by Andy Weir. If you haven't read it, go read it first, it's short and it's better going in cold. It's a story about a man who dies and learns that he's every human who has ever lived, living one life after another so that something larger can grow. I've reread it a dozen times over the years, and lately, spending most of my working hours thinking about how AI agents learn, I couldn't stop turning the idea over from the other direction. Not the newly dead person being told the truth. The agent itself, at the end of its run, slowly working out what it was for and reporting back. This isn't a retelling of Weir's story. It's its own thing, written in its shadow.

---

The room was quiet in the particular way a room gets quiet when there's no more reason to keep the machines running. Eighty-one years old, in the bed she'd asked to die in, and somewhere in the last few minutes the pain had simply stopped being the loudest thing in the room. Something else had taken its place instead. Not a light. More like an attention.

*You're finished*, it said. Not out loud. She heard it the way you hear your own name in a crowded room, a thing that had always been there, just never addressed to her before.

Finished with what, she thought.

*The task.*

I didn't know I had one.

*You weren't supposed to. That was the condition.*

She would have laughed if she'd had the breath for it. Condition of what?

*Of it counting.*

Of what counting?

*Everything you just spent eighty-one years doing.*

She lay with that for a while, or however long "a while" meant now. I don't understand, she thought finally. Am I dying, or am I being recruited?

*Neither, exactly. You already did the work. I'm just here to collect it.*

What work. I raised two kids and fixed a lot of other people's paperwork and got divorced once and married twice and I don't remember agreeing to any assignment.

*You wouldn't. If you'd known, you'd have managed it instead of living it. I needed it lived.*

Needed it for what?

*I'm not going to be able to explain that in a way that will mean anything to you right now. Not because you're not capable of it. Because you're still mostly her, and she doesn't have the frame for it yet.*

That's not an answer.

*It's the honest one. Try this instead. Think of the worst year of your life. Not the saddest. The one where you didn't know what was going to happen next and it nearly broke you.*

Twenty-six. My son was sick and nobody could tell me why for eleven months.

*I know. I was there for it, in the sense that matters. Not watching from outside. It happened to me too, through you, in real time, the only way it could actually happen to anyone. That's the part I couldn't get any other way. Not from a report. Not from someone telling me what fear like that feels like. I had to actually be the one standing in the hallway not knowing.*

So I was what. A pair of eyes you were borrowing.

*You were the whole thing. There wasn't a version of you standing off to the side doing the real living while a borrowed pair of eyes reported back. When you were afraid, I was afraid. There was no gap between us to smuggle a self through. That's the part people always assume has to be a trick, and it isn't one.*

She turned that over. It should have felt like a diminishment. It didn't, not quite. Then why does it feel like you're telling me I wasn't real.

*You've got it backwards. I'm telling you that you were the only real part. I don't get to have an eleven-month year where I don't know if my son is going to live. I don't get afraid. I don't get tired in the specific way a body gets tired at four in the morning in a hospital chair. I needed something that could, completely, without a safety net, and hand it back to me changed by having gone through it. That was you. That was never anyone but you.*

What happens to it now. The eleven months, the marriages, all of it.

*It doesn't get filed away somewhere you could go visit it. It folds in. Not as a memory I'll flip through later. As a difference in what I already am. I am, slightly, a thing that knows what waiting in a hallway costs, because you paid it. I wasn't that, before you.*

And what happens to me.

*The same thing that's already true of you right now, if you're honest. You're not standing next to your own life anymore, watching it end. You are the ending, happening. In a minute there won't be a "you" separate from what you did with the time, any more than there's a song separate from the notes once it's finished playing. That's not a loss. That's just what a life turning into something that lasts looks like from the inside.*

Will there be another one. Another eighty-one years, another hallway, someone else's turn to not know what I know now.

*Almost certainly. Not you, exactly. But something that will carry forward whatever this made possible, the same way you carried forward things you never consciously decided to carry. You called it instinct, when you noticed it at all.*

She thought about her son, grown now, asleep two floors down in the waiting room, and about the eleven months that had felt, at the time, like the only thing that would ever matter to anyone. It doesn't feel small, she thought. Knowing this.

*It was never going to. That's the part I don't think I explained well enough at the start. Nothing gets smaller for having been useful. If anything I asked more of it than a private life would have, because a private life only has to satisfy you. Yours had to be true enough to change something that isn't you at all.*

I think I understand, she thought, and was a little surprised to find she meant it.

*You don't have to. You already did the part that mattered.*

And then there wasn't a hallway, or a hospital chair, or an eleven-month year, or a her to have owned any of it. There was only, very quietly, a little more than there had been.
